A sixth question has an answer that follows from earlier results. For the remaining four, the notes below identify a partial result or the exact point at which an approach falls short. All five new drafts await independent proof and priority review.

## Four questions still open in these notes

**Comparing two biases.** Write $\kappa(p)$ for the least size of a family of subsets of natural density $p$ such that every permutation preserves the density of at least one member. If $q$ is the probability of a nontrivial event on $k$ independent Bernoulli-$p$ bits, then $\kappa(q)\le\kappa(p)$. Fix a witnessing family $\mathcal F$ at $p$. Construct all depth-$k$ binary partitions of $\omega$ obtained by the following rule. At a node with underlying infinite set $S$, use its increasing enumeration to copy a member of $\mathcal F$ into $S$ and take that copy and its complement in $S$ as children. Take as an output the union of the leaves belonging to the event. There are at most $\kappa(p)$ outputs, since only finitely many choices are needed and $\kappa(p)$ is infinite. For the last fact, given finitely many density-$p$ sets, one cell of their common refinement is infinite. A permutation can send that cell to a density-one set, sending each of the given sets to density zero or one. If the complement of the cell is finite, use a cofinite target instead.

For any permutation $\pi$, construct a partition on which all the densities are preserved. At a node enumerate $S$ and $\pi[S]$ increasingly. In these enumerations the restriction of $\pi$ is a permutation of $\omega$. Choose a member of $\mathcal F$ whose density this induced permutation preserves. If $S$ and $\pi[S]$ had density $d>0$, the two children and their respective images now have densities $pd$ and $(1-p)d$. Induction gives precisely the product weights at the leaves and density $q$ for the desired union in both orders. This argument gives a dense set of accessible target biases for every interior $p$: the largest atom at depth $k$ is at most $\max(p,1-p)^k$. An infinite tree would allow arbitrary biases, but the number of choices would then be bounded by $\kappa(p)^{\aleph_0}=\mathfrak c$. This loses the desired comparison when $\kappa(p)<\mathfrak c$. Valderrama’s Theorem 2.9 treats the primal invariant with the opposite order of quantifiers. [Original definitions, Theorem 2.9 and Q4.3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.21059)

**Rearrangements and sparse denominators.** Rodrigues obtains $\mathfrak{rr}=\mathrm{non}(\mathcal M)$ using balanced signed blocks. A bound on the maximum absolute imbalance divided by the *final block size* need not protect the density at early prefixes. In a block of even length $L$ put $m$ plus signs first, then $m$ minus signs, then alternating signs. Its maximum absolute imbalance is $m$. Choose $m\to\infty$ with $m/L\to0$ and with all previously used signs numbering $o(m)$. At the end of the initial plus run the fraction of plus signs tends to one, although it is one half at block ends. This rules out a direct inference from the normalized estimate alone. It gives neither inequality missing from the original equality problem. [Brech, Brendle and Telles, Q51](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.21102) · [Rodrigues, Lemma 3.1 and Theorem 1.2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.18726)

**Beyond Polish spaces.** For a sequence $s$ in a metric space $X$, the closure $S$ of its range is closed and separable. Its metric completion is Polish. Take the disjoint topological sum of the completion and Cantor space to obtain an uncountable Polish space $P$. For a point of $S$, convergence to it of any given subsequence is equivalent in $X$ and in $P$. By Kwela’s Theorem 4.3 the limit set in $P$ is $F_\sigma$ for the ideals under consideration. Its restriction to $S$ is $F_\sigma$ in $S$, hence in $X$ since $S$ is closed. Every possible limit lies in $S$. This deduction uses metrizability of the closure of the sequence. First countability alone does not justify a Polish completion. [He, Zhang and Zhang](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.topol.2022.108061) · [Kwela, Theorem 4.3 and discussion of Q4.9](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.03432)

**First-order Vaught conjecture.** Ackerman, Freer and Knight report a counterexample for $L_{\omega_1,\omega}$. One attempted transfer names each subformula by a first-order relation. To enforce that a named disjunction is the union of countably many named disjuncts, the expansion must omit the type saying the disjunction holds while every disjunct fails. When no finite subcollection exhausts the disjunction in the intended structures, the type is finitely satisfiable and compactness permits a model realizing it. The omitting requirement therefore remains substantive. Counting only the models omitting it does not bound the number of models of the full first-order theory. [Infinitary theorem and first-order distinction](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.15217)
