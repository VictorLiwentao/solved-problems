# Rainbow Ramsey for triples implies König’s lemma without choice

Wentao Li · Research draft with OpenAI Codex assistance · November 22, 2026

[Proof (PDF)](rainbow-implies-konig.pdf) · [LaTeX source](rainbow-implies-konig.tex) · [BibTeX](citation.bib) · [Finite group check](verify.py)

Banerjee, Gopaulsingh and Molnár ask whether the global bounded rainbow Ramsey theorem implies König’s lemma in ZF or ZFA. Forster and Truss studied the corresponding choice question for ordinary Ramsey colorings. The proof here uses the equality relation of a bounded coloring. [Question 8.3, 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.02215) · [Forster and Truss, 2007](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00153-006-0025-z)

**Theorem.** In ZF and in ZFA, for every finite $m\ge3$ and $b\ge2$, the global principle $\mathrm{RRT}^m_b$ implies countable choice for finite sets, hence König’s lemma. Here $\mathrm{RRT}^m_b$ says that on every infinite set, every coloring of its $m$-element subsets with at most $b$ inputs of each color is injective on all the $m$-element subsets of some infinite subset. König’s lemma concerns arbitrary infinite, locally finite, connected graphs. For $m=2$ and $b\ge3$ we also prove that, under the rainbow hypothesis, König’s lemma is equivalent to countable choice for pairs. The unconditional implication for pairs remains open in this argument.

**A finite coloring independent of the names of the points.** Let $E$ be a nonempty finite set of size $d$ and $T$ the set of its enumerations, the bijections from $d=\{0,\ldots,d-1\}$ to $E$. We construct an equivalence relation on $[T]^3$ with classes of size at most two. It will have the following property: if $S\subseteq T$ is nonempty and rainbow, the group of permutations of $E$ carrying $S$ onto itself has size at most three. The construction is invariant under all renamings of $E$.

Write $G=\mathrm{Sym}(d)$ in its usual lexicographic order on lists of images. For a triple $U$ of enumerations, associate the subgroup $K$ generated by $t^{-1}u$ for $u\in U$, with any $t\in U$. This does not depend on $t$: changing the base multiplies the list on the left by the inverse of one of its elements, and the new list generates the same group. For a triple $A$ in $G$ use the same rule. If $A$ contains the identity its associated subgroup is just $\langle A\rangle$. A triple is called free here if its stabilizer under left multiplication is trivial.

For each $K\le G$ fix at most one of the following rules, using the least admissible finite data in the order on $G$ and preferring the first rule. If neither applies use no rule.

1. If $K$ has an involution $a$ and a free triple $A\subseteq K$ containing the identity and generating $K$, pair $tA$ with $taA$ for every $t\in T$. The map $t\mapsto tA$ is injective, so this defines disjoint pairs.
2. If $K$ has two free triples $A,B\subseteq K$ containing the identity, generating $K$ and lying in distinct left-translation orbits, pair $tA$ with $tB$ for every $t\in T$.

Give each pair its own color and each remaining triple its own color, using the equivalence classes themselves. All translates in either rule have associated subgroup $K$, so rules for different subgroups never overlap. No basis or enumeration of $E$ was selected.

Suppose $S$ is nonempty and its relabeling stabilizer has order greater than three. Fix $t\in S$ temporarily. In the coordinates supplied by $t$, the stabilizer is a subgroup $H\le G$, and $tH\subseteq S$. We show that $H$ contains a subgroup $K$ to which a rule applies. All patterns of that rule lie in $K$, so $tK\subseteq S$ contains both matched triples.

* If $H$ has an involution $a$, take $h\in H\setminus\{1,a\}$, $K=\langle a,h\rangle$ and $A=\{1,a,h\}$. A nontrivial left stabilizer of a triple acts freely and must have order three. A triple containing the identity with such a stabilizer equals that order-three subgroup, impossible here.
* If $H$ has odd order and contains $g$ of order at least five, let $K=\langle g\rangle$. The triples $\{1,g,g^2\}$ and $\{1,g,g^3\}$ generate $K$ and are free. Each contains $1,g$, ruling out a coset of an order-three subgroup. The first has two ordered pairs $(u,v)$ with $u^{-1}v=g$ and the second has just one. They cannot be translates.
* Otherwise $H$ has odd order and all nonidentity elements have order three. Take $g\ne1$ and $h\notin\langle g\rangle$ in $H$. Both $\{1,g,h\}$ and $\{1,g,h^{-1}\}$ generate $K=\langle g,h\rangle$. In each the only ordered pair of right difference $g$ is $(1,g)$. A left translation between the triples would fix $1$ and thus be the identity, while $h\ne h^{-1}$. The same distinguished ordered pair proves freeness.

These cases exhaust the possibilities by the finite group theorem of Cauchy. Thus the stabilizer bound holds.

**Recovering finite lists of candidates.** Given a sequence $(D_i)_{i<\omega}$ of nonempty finite sets, put $E_n=\prod_{i\le n}D_i$. Finite choice suffices to see that each $E_n$ is nonempty. Take $X$ to be the tagged union of the finite nonempty enumeration sets $T_n$ of $E_n$. It is infinite since it projects onto $\omega$. For $m=3$ use the triple coloring on each level and give all other inputs singleton colors. For $m>3$ apply the following rule to an $m$-set whose least occupied level contains exactly three points and whose other points lie at distinct higher levels. Record the least level, the color of its triple, and the exact set of the other points, with a common tag. Give every other $m$-set a separate color. These colorings are 2-bounded.

By the assumed rainbow principle some infinite $Y\subseteq X$ is rainbow. It meets unboundedly many levels, since a finite union of finite levels is finite. On any occupied level the set $S_n=Y\cap T_n$ is rainbow for triples: two colliding triples could be padded by the same $m-3$ points at distinct later occupied levels. This requires only finitely many choices for any one collision.

For each such $n$ and each $t\in S_n$, code $S_n$ by the finite list of permutations $t^{-1}u$ for $u\in S_n$, sorted lexicographically. Take all $t$ giving the least list. Two enumerations giving the same list differ on the left by a relabeling preserving $S_n$. Hence these least-list enumerations form a nonempty set of size at most three by the finite lemma. Take their first values $t(0)\in E_n$, giving a definable nonempty $P_n\subseteq E_n$ of size at most three on unboundedly many $n$.

**Bounded ambiguity gives choice.** More generally, suppose nonempty $P_n\subseteq\prod_{i\le n}D_i$ of size at most $k$ are given on an unbounded $N\subseteq\omega$, with $k$ a fixed positive integer. We prove by finite induction on $k$ that the $D_i$ have a choice function. If for every $i$ some $n\in N$, $n\ge i$, has all members of $P_n$ agreeing at $i$, use their common value at the least such $n$. This includes $k=1$. Otherwise fix a failing coordinate $i$. For every eligible $n$ the projection of $P_n$ there contains at least two elements. Since $D_i$ is finite, some $a\in D_i$ occurs on unboundedly many of those levels. Retain those levels and only their candidates with value $a$ at $i$. The sizes now lie between one and $k-1$, so induction applies. This uses no countable iteration of choices.

Countable choice for finite sets yields the graph version of König’s lemma directly. Fix a root in an infinite connected locally finite graph. Each finite-distance ball is finite. Choose an order on each finite sphere using countable choice for finite sets. The tree of geodesics from the root has finite branching and paths of arbitrarily large finite length. Recursively take the first successor that admits arbitrarily long extensions to produce an infinite ray.

**What happens for pairs with multiplicity at least three.** Under $\mathrm{RRT}^2_b$ for any $b\ge3$, König’s lemma is equivalent to countable choice for pairs. One direction follows by applying König’s lemma to the tree of finite partial choices. For the other, we modify the coloring of an enumeration set $T$ to reduce its symmetry to an elementary abelian 2-group.

An edge $\{t,tu\}$ has associated cyclic group $\langle u\rangle$, unchanged by using the other endpoint. For each cyclic $K\le G$ of order $d\ge3$, choose its least generator $g$. If $d$ is even, pair each left translate of $\{1,g\}$ with the corresponding translate of $g^{d/2}\{1,g\}$. The edge is free under left multiplication, and this matching is involutive. If $d\ge5$ is odd, pair the translates of $\{1,g\}$ with the corresponding translates of $\{1,g^2\}$. These free edge orbits are different and $g^2$ generates the same $K$. If $d=3$, give the three edges in each translate of the triangle $K$ one common color. Use separate colors for all these classes and singleton colors elsewhere. This is 3-bounded and intrinsic on $T$. Any subgroup underlying an orbit in a rainbow set can have no element of order greater than two, since the orbit would contain a whole translate of the corresponding $K$. A finite group of exponent dividing two is elementary abelian.

Use this coloring level by level on the same $X$, with pairs between levels given singleton colors. On each occupied level retain the nonempty set $L_n$ of enumerations yielding the least code for $S_n$. For any $t\in L_n$, the preceding stabilizer argument shows $L_n=tH_n$ for an elementary abelian subgroup $H_n$ of the ordered permutation group on the positions. In detail, the relabeling stabilizer acts transitively and freely on the least-code enumerations, so $H_n$ is that stabilizer in $t$-coordinates. Its description $t^{-1}L_n$ is independent of $t\in L_n$. Give this finite vector space over the two-element field its lexicographically first ordered basis. For each basis coordinate, its zero-coordinate subgroup has index two and its two right-action orbits on $L_n$ form a pair. The pairs, tagged by level and coordinate, make a countable family. Countable choice for pairs selects one orbit from each. At each level the selected orbits intersect in exactly one enumeration, by the independence of the coordinate functionals. The statement also holds when the basis is empty and $L_n$ is a singleton. The first values of these enumerations give partial choices on unboundedly many levels and hence a choice for all the $D_i$.

Thus for pair colorings with multiplicity at least three the remaining implication is precisely whether the rainbow principle implies countable choice for pairs. The three-edge class for an order-three subgroup explains why this reduction has the stated multiplicity assumption.

**Verification.** The accompanying finite check enumerates the triple matching on three symmetric groups, two elementary abelian groups and two odd cyclic groups. It checks relabeling invariance, disjoint color classes and subgroup collisions. The general finite lemma, the choice argument and the pair reduction are proved above.

**Scope and priority.** The implication holds simultaneously as a ZF theorem schema for every finite arity from three upward and every finite multiplicity from two upward. The triple construction uses two independent permutation differences. An unordered pair has only a single difference up to inversion, leaving the pair case unresolved. Proposed significance **69/100** for deriving a standard choice principle from the global rainbow theorem across these arities and reducing the pair case at multiplicities of at least three to choice for pairs.
