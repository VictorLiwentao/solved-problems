# The support test for ideal-characterized circle subgroups

Wentao Li · Research draft with OpenAI Codex assistance · November 22, 2026

[Proof (PDF)](bohr-support-test.pdf) · [LaTeX source](bohr-support-test.tex) · [BibTeX](citation.bib)

For which ideals $I$ does $I$-convergence of $a_nx$ to zero for every $x$ on the circle force the support of the integer sequence $a$ to belong to $I$? Dikranjan, Das and Bose posed the question. Filipów, Kwela, Leonetti and Tryba ask again in Question 2.19, including the case of the density-zero ideal. We give an exact Katětov obstruction and a direct measure test that covers all nonpathological analytic $P$-ideals, including density zero. [Dikranjan, Das and Bose, 2020, §3](https://www.impan.pl/en/publishing-house/journals-and-series/fundamenta-mathematicae/all/249/2/113188/statistically-characterized-subgroups-of-the-circle) · [Filipów, Kwela, Leonetti and Tryba, 2026, Question 2.19 and Remark 2.20](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.12192)

**The obstruction.** On the positive integers let $\mathcal B$ consist of the sets missing some Bohr neighborhood of zero. Such a neighborhood has the form
$$ \{m\ge1:\ \|mx_j\|<\epsilon\text{ for }1\le j\le k\},\qquad x_j\in\mathbb T,\quad\epsilon>0. $$
These neighborhoods have the finite intersection property and are infinite: the pigeonhole principle on the finite-dimensional torus gives arbitrarily good positive returns of $(x_1,\ldots,x_k)$ to zero, with multiples of an exact return available in the periodic case. Each finite set misses some neighborhood, by using one irrational coordinate and choosing $\epsilon$ small enough. Thus $\mathcal B$ is a proper ideal. It is generated by the sets $\{m:\|mx\|\ge\epsilon\}$.

For every ideal $I$ on $\omega$ containing the finite sets, its support test holds precisely when
$$ \mathcal B\not\le_K I\mathbin{\restriction}A \quad\text{for every } A\notin I. $$
Indeed, for an integer sequence with positive support $A$ the map $f(n)=|a_n|$ on $A$ is a reduction exactly when the preimage of each generator lies in $I$. That says exactly that $I\text{-}\lim_n a_n x=0$ for every $x$. Conversely extend any reduction by zero off $A$ to obtain a sequence violating the support test. If the support lies in $I$, convergence is immediate. This identifies the single obstruction ideal explicitly. It is the positive-integer version of the whole-circle ideal in the source's Theorem 2.11 and Remark 2.20. The criterion by itself recodes convergence.

**A measure test.** Suppose every positive $A$ supports a sequence of finitely supported probability measures $\nu_j$ with $\nu_j(B)\to0$ for each $B\in I$. The support test then holds. If $a_n\ne0$ on $A$ and convergence held for every circle point, we would have
$$ h_j(x)=\sum_{n\in A}\nu_j(\{n\})\mathbf1_{\{\|a_nx\|\ge1/4\}}\longrightarrow0 $$
at every $x$. Dominated convergence would give $\int h_j\to0$ for Haar probability. Yet multiplication by each nonzero integer preserves Haar probability, so every integral equals $1/2$.

For the density-zero ideal take uniform measures on $A\cap[0,N_j)$ along endpoints where $|A\cap[0,N_j)|/N_j$ stays bounded away from zero. This also gives a short direct proof in the particular case the authors singled out.

More generally write $I=\mathrm{Exh}(\phi)$, with $\phi$ a lower semicontinuous submeasure that is nonpathological, meaning it is the pointwise supremum of countably additive measures bounded by $\phi$. For $A\notin I$ choose $c>0$ with all its tail submeasures greater than $2c$. For each $j$ choose a finite $F_j\subseteq A\setminus[0,j)$ with $\phi(F_j)>2c$, and a dominated measure assigning $F_j$ mass greater than $c$. Restrict it to $F_j$ and normalize to mass one. Singleton masses can be taken finite, as in the usual representation by lower semicontinuous submeasures finite on finite sets. The resulting $\nu_j$ satisfies $\nu_j(B)\le\phi(B\setminus[0,j))/c\to0$ for $B\in I$. The measure test applies.

**Priority.** The 2020 paper already expected the density-zero answer. The 2026 paper gives its own sufficient condition involving the eventually-different ideal, and a counterexample for another ideal. The use of dominating measures here gives a broad sufficient class and explains the role of Haar measure. The test does not assert necessity of nonpathologicality.
